\## Where the Spinner Fits



| Aspect | Assessment |

|--------|------------|

| \*\*Primary function\*\* | Reaction wheel (attitude control) |

| \*\*Secondary function\*\* | Gravity-gradient energy harvesting (μ-scale) |

| \*\*Relation to A\*\* | A uses magnetic field (strong); S uses gravity gradient (weak) — different domains |

| \*\*Relation to B\*\* | S is a specific implementation of B — a reaction wheel with regenerative braking |

| \*\*Relation to C\*\* | Both use resonance. C harvests vibrations; S harvests gravity gradient. |



The spinner is not a competitor to A, B, or C. It is a \*\*special case\*\* of B (reaction wheel) with a \*\*small additional harvesting capability\*\* from the gravity gradient. The harvesting is not commercially significant, but it is scientifically interesting.

\## Energy Scale Comparison



| Method | Energy per cycle (typical) | Power (continuous) |

|--------|---------------------------|-------------------|

| A (ETERP) | >10 kJ (per orbit) | >10 W |

| B (RWERS) | >100 kJ (per desaturation) | Not continuous |

| C (RAEH) | 1–1000 J (per cycle) | 0.1–10 W |

| S (Spinner) | \~0.002–0.02 J (per orbit) | \~1 nW – 1 μW |



The spinner's energy scale is \*\*4–10 orders of magnitude smaller\*\* than the other methods. This is not a failure. It is a \*\*calibration\*\* — showing that gravity-gradient harvesting is not practical for power, but may be useful for sensing or stabilization.

\## Conclusion



A, B, and C are \*\*practical engineering solutions\*\* ready for deployment or near-deployment. The spinner is a \*\*research prototype\*\* — valuable for asking the question, not for powering a satellite.



Publishing them together provides \*\*honest context\*\*. The spinner does not compete with these methods. It complements them by exploring a different physical regime — one that is weak, but not zero.
